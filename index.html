The official story

Everything went up.
Wages kept up.

The BLS has tracked consumer prices since 1919. Their CPI-U is the benchmark for Social Security COLAs, tax bracket indexing, and wage negotiations across the entire economy.

And by this measure, the story is actually fine. Wages rose +877% since 1971. CPI rose +900%. You're almost exactly keeping pace with everything in the basket.

The problem

CPI was never designed to track what you're trying to build.

CPI measures the cost of consuming — groceries, gasoline, rent, appliances. It was never designed to track the cost of building a life you no longer have to work for.

"By design, the CPI excludes every asset class entirely. Not as an oversight — it's the stated purpose. The flaw is using CPI to answer a question it was never asked."

A home. A retirement account. Equities. Gold. The inputs to generational wealth. None appear in CPI. Zero percent weight. By design.

The extraction gap

You got 75% more productive.
Your real wage barely moved.

Since 1971, U.S. worker output per hour rose +175% in real terms. Real median wages over the same period: nearly flat. The gap between what workers produced and what they captured is the extraction — and it had to land somewhere.

Where did the extraction go? Productivity gains that didn't flow to wages flowed instead to corporate profits — then into dividends, buybacks, and retained earnings. That capital found its way into equities, real estate, and hard assets. This is why MHIM's Tier 1 (assets) rose +4,491% while real wages stayed flat: the productivity gap and the asset inflation gap are the same phenomenon, measured from opposite sides of the ledger. Workers created the value. Capital captured it as price appreciation.

⚖️ The strongest counterargument

The raw gap overstates extraction for several reasons. First, employer healthcare costs count as compensation but not as take-home wages — an honest comparison should add these back. Second, productivity and wages are often deflated using different price indexes (GDP deflator vs. CPI), which mechanically widens the gap. Third, the shift from manufacturing to services changes what "output per hour" means. EPI's best estimate after these adjustments still finds a 60–70% unexplained gap — substantial, but smaller than the raw numbers suggest. The labor share of income (workers' cut of all output) has fallen from ~65% in 1971 to ~56% today; that 9-point decline is the least contested measure of the shift.

The 3.5× gap isn't an error.
It's a category.

CPI and MHIM aren't disagreeing about prices. They're answering different questions. CPI asks: what does it cost to keep consuming? MHIM asks: what does it cost to build the life most people are actually working toward — one where work eventually becomes optional?

Official CPI — BLS methodology
Housing (36%) — Owner's Equivalent Rent: survey of what homeowners think they'd charge for rent. Not actual purchase prices (replaced in 1983).
Healthcare (7%) — Tracks insurer's retained earnings margin, not the premium you pay.
Education (6%) — Treated as optional discretionary spending.
Assets (0%) — Excluded entirely and intentionally.
Formula — Geometric mean + substitution: if steak inflates and you switch to hamburger, steak inflation stops counting.
MHIM — this site's methodology
Assets (40%) — Actual FRED home prices, S&P 500, gold, silver, farmland. Entry price to the ownership economy.
Necessities (45%) — Actual KFF family premiums, actual tuition, food staples, rent, gasoline.
Discretionary (15%) — New cars, consumer electronics. Includes what genuinely deflated. Honesty requires it.
Formula — Fixed-weight arithmetic basket. No substitution, no hedonic adjustments.

⚖️ The strongest steelman for CPI

CPI's choices have technical merit. Counting both a full health insurance premium and the underlying medical services would double-count. OER keeps the index about shelter costs rather than investment returns. For a household focused purely on consumption with no plan to build wealth, CPI may genuinely be closer to lived experience. MHIM is explicit about what it measures: the cost of the wealth-building trajectory — not the cost of subsistence.
